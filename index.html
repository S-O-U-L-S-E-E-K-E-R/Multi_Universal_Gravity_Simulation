A testable multiverse idea

Marbles on the Sheet

Picture every universe as a marble resting on one shared sheet of space. We can't see past our own marble's edge, because light from beyond it never reaches us. But the pull of other marbles could still reach across the sheet into ours.

The theory makes three claims. This page simulates each one and checks it against real numbers.

  • Dark matter is other universes. The extra gravity we measure in galaxies comes from neighbouring marbles, not unseen particles.
  • Extreme density makes big bangs. Wherever matter from the universes piles up densely enough, it ignites as a new universe.
  • Our edges are pulled unevenly. Neighbours close to our edge tug harder on that side.
Simulation 1 · the sheet Scale: whole universes

The multiverse on a flat sheet

Each universe is a collection of weights (the dots) that move freely; it isn't one solid object. Every universe starts with its own big bang. Its matter is packed densely at the centre and thinly at the edge, and all of it flies outward, so the universe's edge grows over time and its centre thins out. Every weight pulls on every other weight, in its own universe and in the others. Nothing is held in a fixed shape.

Neighbours can drag a universe's thin outer matter, denting it where dense matter pulls inward and stretching it elsewhere, like dense pieces pressed into expanding play-dough. The outline around each universe shows its current shape. Wherever matter packs together tightly enough, whether gravity pulls it together or it crashes in from two universes, it ignites as a newborn universe with its own big bang. Density alone sets it off. The "densest patch" counter shows how close the sheet is getting. Total mass never changes. Switch to the dented view to see the wells in 3D.

Test 1 · the dark-matter claim, edges close together Scale: one galaxy, neighbours at universe scale

Can neighbours touching our edge hold a galaxy together?

This is the strongest evidence for dark matter. Stars at the edge of a galaxy orbit far too fast for the visible mass to hold them. The measured speed stays flat at about 200 km/s instead of dropping off. If neighbour universes are the cause, their pull has to supply the missing inward force.

Here everything sits on a flat sheet. Our universe is the teal disc, 46.5 billion light-years in radius, and the neighbours are the orange discs. Each universe's matter is spread across its whole disc, not packed into a point. Nothing says neighbours have to be far away, since we couldn't see one even if it sat right at our horizon. The edge gap starts at zero, with the neighbours touching us, and set a gravity reach so that distant matter stops counting. Each dot is a galaxy, coloured by how much of its missing pull the neighbours supply. Click any galaxy to see its rotation curve.

What if a neighbour has expanded right through our galaxy?

Once an expanded neighbour overlaps our space, some of its matter sits inside each galaxy's orbits, and that matter does pull inward. Test 1 couldn't capture this. The catch is that an expanded universe is spread very thin. This test fills the galaxy with the neighbour's matter at a density you choose, measured against the average density of our own universe (about 4 × 10⁻²⁸ kg per cubic metre, roughly one hydrogen atom in every 4 cubic metres).

Test 2 · a neighbour's matter inside our galaxy Scale: one galaxy

What if a neighbour universe has expanded into our space?

In Simulation 1, expanding universes grow into each other and overlap. Where a neighbour overlaps us, its matter is in our space. If that matter only interacts through gravity (it gives off no light we can see and passes through our matter), it falls into our galaxies' wells and gathers as a cloud of neighbour matter around each one. Stars orbiting in our galaxy then feel mass inside their orbit, which is what Test 1 showed is required.

Simulation 2 · galaxies forming with a neighbour's matter Scale: many galaxies inside one universe

Does the neighbour's matter gather around our galaxies on its own?

Test 2 only works if the neighbour's matter sits around each of our galaxies, heavier and more spread out. This simulation checks whether that happens on its own. Our matter and the neighbour's matter share the same space but start out unrelated: each is almost smooth, with its own independent random lumps. They interact only through gravity: no collisions, no light. Any lining-up has to come from gravity alone.

Our matter behaves like real gas: it can shed energy and sink to the centre of a well. You choose whether the neighbour matter can do the same. Untick Gravity between them to run the control experiment.

Test suite · can other universes do dark matter's job? Scale: galaxies and galaxy clusters

Seven versions, five measured tests

Each version of the idea gets its best settings from galaxy rotation alone. It then has to pass every other test with those same settings, with no refitting. The targets are all measured: the link between visible and actual pull across 153 galaxies, the rule that a galaxy's outer speed⁴ grows with its visible mass, about 6× hidden mass in galaxy clusters, and the Bullet Cluster's hidden mass sitting with its galaxies instead of its gas.

What this shows

Only one version passes all five tests: a neighbour universe's matter that has gathered in our galaxies' wells (D-b). Checked on held-out galaxies it still matched 88–95% of them, with the same settings, though its speed–mass slope sat at the low edge (3.5–3.6).
It needs one thing my simulation doesn't make on its own: small galaxies losing part of their ordinary matter (thought to be blown out by star explosions). Without that (D-a, exactly as Simulation 2 forms it), the speed–mass rule fails.
Gravity leaking below a fixed acceleration (E2) matches galaxies almost perfectly, because it's MOND, but it fails clusters and the Bullet Cluster. Neighbours beside us, being inside a bigger well, and overlapping neighbours fail every test.

What the real galaxies say

Neighbour matter in our wells works on real galaxies, cutting the miss from 0.57 dex (visible matter only) to 0.18. Keeping it out of each galaxy's very centre (a "core") helps clearly: 64% of galaxies fit better with a core, and in 20,000 resamples the core won every time.
But one core size for every galaxy is ruled out. Fitted one by one, even with each galaxy's star mass free, a single common core size fits only 48% of them (a true universal one would fit about 68%). Bigger galaxies prefer bigger cores.
MOND-like leaking (E2) still fits rotation curves best (0.17 dex), as in professional work. It just fails clusters and the Bullet Cluster.
Not produced by the simulation

Small galaxies losing gas

This version needs small galaxies to hold relatively more neighbour matter. I added star explosions to Simulation 2: every so often a fixed share of each galaxy's gas gets the same outward kick, and gravity decides whether it escapes. Across 24 random starts at each of four kick strengths, the ratio stayed flat at about 5 for every galaxy size, with slopes from −0.03 to +0.01 (±0.05). It needs about −0.2 to −0.3. Stronger kicks mostly destroyed galaxies instead of creating a trend. So this ingredient still has to be added by hand. The simulation's galaxies only span a factor of about 4 in mass (real ones span thousands), which limits the test.

Suggestive, not established

Core sizes that vary by place

If the neighbour's matter is spread like a "wavy sea", thicker and calmer in some regions than others, each galaxy picks up the local conditions. That predicts something standard dark matter doesn't: galaxies near each other in space should have similar core sizes, whatever their own size.

Tested: I matched sky positions (CDS resolver) and SPARC distances to the 88 galaxies with a pinned-down core size and removed the size trend. Galaxies within 5–20 Mpc of each other have core sizes about 25% more similar than random pairs (p = 0.003 after correcting for trying several scales). But core size also leans slightly with distance from us, a possible measurement effect. Shuffling only among galaxies at similar distances keeps the same 21–27% effect but drops the significance to p = 0.11. A larger rotation-curve sample could settle it.

What the simulation says

Results, claim by claim

Fails, even edge to edge

Neighbours beside us on the sheet

Too weak. Even with a neighbour's edge touching ours and a galaxy close to our edge, the extra pull is hundreds of thousands of times too small. A universe's mass is spread across billions of light-years, so very little of it is close to any one galaxy.

Wrong shape. Mass outside a galaxy pulls one side and pushes the other. By Gauss's law, the average over a sphere is zero. Dark matter's effect is a net inward pull, and that needs mass inside the orbit.

The speed of gravity isn't the problem. Changes in a well travel at the speed of light. In 2017, gravitational waves and light from the same neutron-star collision arrived 1.7 seconds apart after 130 million years. But a well that was already in place before our universe formed would already reach us, and all the tests here put our galaxies inside the neighbours' wells from the start. Sitting in a well doesn't change orbits by itself: a whole galaxy in a smooth well falls together, and its stars can't feel it. Only differences in the well's slope from one side of a galaxy to the other change orbits, and that difference is what comes out far too weak and lopsided.

A reach limit makes it worse. With a gravity cutoff, the average pull turns outward, as if the neighbour had negative mass.

Overlapping doesn't save it. A neighbour expanded through our galaxy would need to be about 200,000 times denser than our universe. Even then the curve keeps climbing instead of staying flat, because evenly spread matter adds more pull the farther out you go. A flat curve needs matter that thins out with distance from each galaxy's centre.

Can match the data

A neighbour's matter in our wells

Where a neighbour universe has expanded into our space, its matter is here too. If it only interacts through gravity, it falls into our galaxies' wells. That cloud fits the measured curve to within a few km/s if it carries roughly 15 to 25 times a galaxy's visible mass, spread about 15 times wider. A cloud the same shape as our galaxy doesn't work.

That is almost exactly the dark-matter halo astronomers already infer, but made of another universe's matter. It matches lensing maps such as the Bullet Cluster's, because matter that only feels gravity passes straight through collisions.

On 149 real galaxies it cuts the rotation-curve miss from 0.57 to 0.18 dex, and a core at each galaxy's centre helps clearly. But one core size for every galaxy is ruled out, and the gas loss it needs in small galaxies didn't come out of the simulation on its own.

And it gathers on its own. In Simulation 2, with the neighbour's matter sharing our space, gravity lines the two kinds of matter up almost perfectly (about 0.95 on a 0–1 scale), even though they start with unrelated lumps. With gravity between them switched off, they stay unrelated (about 0) and no galaxies form at all. Our galaxies end up at the centres of neighbour-matter clouds about 5–10 times wider, and the rotation curves come out flatter (0.60–0.72 vs 0.46–0.49 for our matter alone). It needs the neighbour to be several times denser than our universe, and its matter must not cool and sink the way ours does.

Testable prediction

Uneven pull on our edges

Neighbours close to some sides of our universe would stretch it more along one axis. On the sheet this gets stronger over time: as our universe expands and its centre weakens, we end up sitting inside our neighbours' wells, and they sit inside ours. That predicts expansion running slightly faster in some directions, and a matching distortion in the largest patterns of the cosmic microwave background (CMB).

There are disputed hints of this: the CMB "axis of evil", a claimed "dark flow" of galaxy clusters, and direction-dependent Hubble measurements. A neighbour's tidal pull would stretch us along one axis, so these should share an axis.

Checked against published directions: six anomaly axes (the excess radio and quasar dipole, the CosmicFlows-4 bulk flow, the X-ray cluster Hubble-rate anisotropy, the CMB power asymmetry, the axis of evil, and the dark flow) are no more lined up than random directions (p ≈ 0.5). The three motion anomalies do sit within about 20° of one axis at (l, b) ≈ (289°, 3°), but a group that tight shows up among six random axes about half the time. That axis is also near the Great Attractor, a known mass concentration. So the anomalies don't currently support one shared axis. A cleaner test would need each anomaly measured with matching methods.

Plausible, not tested here

Extreme density making big bangs

On the sheet, gravity pulls matter from the expanding universes into dense knots, and any knot dense enough ignites into a newborn universe that expands in turn. The sheet becomes a shifting pattern of strong young wells and weak old ones, which looks like a neural network or a choppy sea. How often this happens depends entirely on the density threshold, which no one knows.

A collision between bubble universes would leave a round, disc-shaped mark on the CMB. Searches of WMAP data (Feeney et al., 2011) found no convincing signal yet.

How it works: Simulation 1 uses softened Newtonian gravity on a wrap-around sheet in made-up units, so it shows behaviour, not real scales. The tests use real SI values. The galaxy has 6 × 10¹⁰ solar masses of visible matter (Hernquist profile, 3 kpc scale). In Test 1 each universe is a flat disc of 1,200 mass points, and the gravity reach uses a Yukawa fall-off. In Test 2 the neighbour-matter cloud is a Hernquist sphere whose pull is softened inside the core size. Simulation 2 is a particle-mesh simulation: 3,136 particles each of our matter and the neighbour's matter on a 128 × 128 wrap-around grid, with softened gravity between all of them (the offset setting keeps the two kinds slightly apart, which softens their pull on each other at short range). Expansion is modelled with expanding-space equations, and gas cooling as friction against the local flow. It runs in made-up units, and anything smaller than about one grid cell isn't resolved. The "measured" curve is a stylised spiral-galaxy curve that flattens near 200 km/s, not a specific dataset.
