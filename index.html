A testable multiverse idea

Marbles on the Sheet

Picture every universe as a marble resting on one shared sheet of space. We can't see past our own marble's edge, because light from beyond it never reaches us. But the pull of other marbles could still reach across the sheet into ours.

The theory makes three claims. This page simulates each one and checks it against real numbers.

  • Dark matter is other universes. The extra gravity we measure in galaxies comes from neighbouring marbles, not unseen particles.
  • Collisions make big bangs. Expanding universes grow into each other, and the matter where they collide forms a dense new universe.
  • Our edges are pulled unevenly. Neighbours close to our edge tug harder on that side.
Simulation 1 · the sheet

The multiverse on a flat sheet

Each universe is a cloud of internal weights (the dots), heavier toward its centre and lighter toward its edge. Every weight pulls on its own, so mass near a neighbour counts for more and mass on the far side counts for less. Every universe expands: its weights spread out, so the pull at its centre keeps getting weaker. Nothing collapses, and total mass never changes. Each universe's edge (the outlined circle) is its observable horizon: the farthest its light can reach. Two universes can sit edge to edge without either seeing the other. Gravity only acts between two universes when their edges are within the gravity reach. Push the reach down, and universes far enough apart stop affecting each other at all. The colours on the sheet show the combined pull: brighter bands are deeper wells.

When two expanding universes grow into each other far enough, the matter where they meet forms a dense newborn universe: a big bang. It takes a share of mass from both parents, starts small with a strong centre, and then expands like the rest. The teal ring shows how unevenly the neighbours pull on our edges, exaggerated so you can see it. Switch to the dented view to see the sheet in 3D.

Test 1 · the dark-matter claim, edges close together

Can neighbours touching our edge hold a galaxy together?

This is the strongest evidence for dark matter. Stars at the edge of a galaxy orbit far too fast for the visible mass to hold them. The measured speed stays flat at about 200 km/s instead of dropping off. If neighbour universes are the cause, their pull has to supply the missing inward force.

Here everything sits on a flat sheet. Our universe is the teal disc, 46.5 billion light-years in radius, and the neighbours are the orange discs. Each universe's matter is spread across its whole disc, not packed into a point. Nothing says neighbours have to be far away, since we couldn't see one even if it sat right at our horizon. The edge gap starts at zero, with the neighbours touching us, and set a gravity reach so that distant matter stops counting. Each dot is a galaxy, coloured by how much of its missing pull the neighbours supply. Click any galaxy to see its rotation curve.

Test 2 · the stacked-sheets version

What if the other universe is stacked under ours?

Now the neighbour isn't beside us on the sheet. It lies on a second sheet just beneath ours, separated by a gap in a hidden direction, like two pages in a book. Its matter clumps under our galaxies. We can't see that matter, but its gravity reaches across the gap. Stars orbiting in our galaxy now feel mass inside their orbit, which is what Test 1 showed is required.

What the simulation says

Results, claim by claim

Fails, even edge to edge

Neighbours beside us on the sheet

Too weak. Even with a neighbour's edge touching ours and a galaxy close to our edge, the extra pull is hundreds of thousands of times too small. A universe's mass is spread across billions of light-years, so very little of it is close to any one galaxy.

Wrong shape. Mass outside a galaxy pulls one side and pushes the other. By Gauss's law, the average over a sphere is zero. Dark matter's effect is a net inward pull, and that needs mass inside the orbit.

The speed of gravity isn't the problem. Changes in a well travel at the speed of light. In 2017, gravitational waves and light from the same neutron-star collision arrived 1.7 seconds apart after 130 million years. But a well that was already in place before our universe formed would already reach us. So in this model, the horizon doesn't block the neighbours' pull. What rules them out is how weak and lopsided that pull is.

A reach limit makes it worse. With a gravity cutoff, the average pull turns outward, as if the neighbour had negative mass.

Can match the data

Stacked sheets

A hidden sheet fits the measured curve to within a few km/s, but only under tight conditions. The sheets must be closer than about 10 kpc. The hidden galaxy must carry roughly 15 to 25 times the visible mass, spread about 15 times wider than our galaxy. An exact copy of our galaxy doesn't work.

That is almost exactly the dark-matter halo astronomers already infer, just living on another sheet. Physicists call this idea brane-world "shadow matter." It matches lensing maps such as the Bullet Cluster's, because the hidden matter passes straight through collisions.

Testable prediction

Uneven pull on our edges

Neighbours close to some sides of our universe would stretch it more along one axis. On the sheet this gets stronger over time: as our universe expands and its centre weakens, we end up sitting inside our neighbours' wells, and they sit inside ours. That predicts expansion running slightly faster in some directions, and a matching distortion in the largest patterns of the cosmic microwave background (CMB).

There are disputed hints of this: the CMB "axis of evil", a claimed "dark flow" of galaxy clusters, and direction-dependent Hubble measurements. Your model predicts they all point the same way, which can be checked.

Plausible, not tested here

Collisions making big bangs

On the sheet, expanding universes keep running into each other, and each collision seeds a compact newborn that expands in turn. The sheet becomes a shifting pattern of strong young wells and weak old ones, which looks like a neural network or a choppy sea.

A collision between bubble universes would leave a round, disc-shaped mark on the CMB. Searches of WMAP data (Feeney et al., 2011) found no convincing signal yet.

How it works: Simulation 1 uses softened Newtonian gravity on a wrap-around sheet in made-up units, so it shows behaviour, not real scales. The tests use real SI values. The galaxy has 6 × 10¹⁰ solar masses of visible matter (Hernquist profile, 3 kpc scale). In Test 1 each universe is a flat disc of 1,200 mass points, and the gravity reach uses a Yukawa fall-off. In Test 2 the hidden galaxy is a Hernquist sphere offset by the sheet separation. The "measured" curve is a stylised spiral-galaxy curve that flattens near 200 km/s, not a specific dataset.
