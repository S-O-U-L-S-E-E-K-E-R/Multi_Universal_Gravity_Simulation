Marbles on the Sheet

Picture every universe as a marble resting on one shared sheet of space. Each marble dents the sheet. We can't see past our own marble's edge, because light from beyond it never reaches us. But the dents from other marbles could still tilt the sheet under us.

The theory makes three claims. This page simulates each one and checks it against real numbers.

  • Dark matter is other universes. The extra gravity we measure in galaxies comes from neighbouring marbles, not unseen particles.
  • Collisions make big bangs. Marbles drift together, merge into dense wells, and those wells become new universes.
  • Our edges are pulled unevenly. Several neighbours tug on different sides of our universe at once.

Test 1 · the dark-matter claim

Can other universes hold a galaxy together?

This is the strongest evidence for dark matter. Stars at the edge of a galaxy orbit far too fast for the visible mass to hold them. The measured speed stays flat at about 200 km/s instead of dropping off. If other universes are the cause, their pull has to supply the missing inward force.

The test puts a Milky Way–sized galaxy inside our universe and surrounds it with 30 neighbour universes in random directions, all beyond our 46.5-billion-light-year horizon. Turn the sliders up and see whether the orange band (stars orbiting in every direction around the disk) ever lies on the flat measured curve.

What the simulation says

Results, claim by claim

Fails as stated

Other universes replacing dark matter

Too weak. With neighbours the size of our observable universe, the extra pull is hundreds of thousands of times too small, even with every neighbour parked just outside our horizon. A universe 60 billion light-years away is far from every galaxy, so its pull is almost identical everywhere in our universe.

Wrong shape. A pull that's the same everywhere moves the whole galaxy at once and can't hold stars in orbit. Only the difference across the galaxy (the tidal part) matters. By Gauss's law, gravity from mass outside a region adds zero net inward pull through any sphere around it. Pull on one side always comes with push on another. Dark matter's effect is a net inward pull, which needs mass inside the orbit.

Wrong place. Lensing maps, such as the Bullet Cluster's, show the hidden mass sitting on each individual galaxy cluster. A distant source would stretch the whole sky in one direction instead.

Plausible, not testable here

Collisions making big bangs

On the sheet, attraction does what you predicted. Marbles gather into filaments and dense wells, and the pattern looks like a neural network or a choppy sea. Our own universe has a similar cosmic web of galaxies, built by gravity.

Physicists have studied a related idea: bubble universes colliding in eternal inflation. A collision would leave a round, disc-shaped mark on the cosmic microwave background (CMB). Searches of WMAP data (Feeney et al., 2011) found no convincing signal. The search is still a good model for how to test this claim.

Testable prediction

Uneven pull on our edges

This is the strongest part of the idea. Neighbours near some sides of our universe would stretch it more along one axis, as the teal ring shows. That predicts expansion that runs slightly faster in some directions, plus a matching distortion in the largest patterns of the CMB.

Astronomers have reported hints of exactly this kind of thing: the CMB "axis of evil" alignment, a claimed "dark flow" of galaxy clusters, and direction-dependent Hubble measurements. All are disputed. Your model would predict that they all point along the same axis, and that can be checked.

A version that could work

How to rescue the dark-matter part

The tests rule out neighbours that are far away across the sheet. They don't rule out a universe that's close to ours in a hidden direction, lying right under our galaxies, the way two sheets of paper stack. Its matter would then pull from inside each galaxy's orbit, which is what the data needs.

Physicists have proposed this as brane-world "shadow matter." To test it, the hidden mass has to follow the visible mass closely, which is a sharper prediction than particle dark matter makes.

How it works: Simulation 1 uses softened Newtonian gravity on a wrap-around sheet in made-up units, so it shows behaviour, not real scales. Test 1 uses real SI values. The galaxy has 6 × 10¹⁰ solar masses of visible matter (Hernquist profile, 3 kpc scale). Each neighbour's tidal pull is computed from its exact distance and direction. The "measured" curve is a stylised spiral-galaxy curve that flattens near 200 km/s, not a specific dataset.
